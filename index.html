Documentation

The microwave, documented

A running record of how a box that heats food became one, written with more seriousness than it deserves.

KB-0011945

The incident

Raytheon engineer Percy Spencer is testing an active magnetron — the tube behind wartime radar — when he notices the candy bar in his pocket has melted. He sends out for popcorn kernels next. They pop. An egg follows, and does not end well for a nearby colleague.

KB-0021945–47

Filing & release

Raytheon files a patent for microwave cooking that October. The first commercial unit, the Radarange, ships a couple of years later: about six feet tall, several hundred pounds, and priced well out of reach of a home kitchen.

KB-003always true

How it actually heats

A magnetron drives water, fat, and sugar molecules — all slightly lopsided electrically — to keep flipping in step with an oscillating field. That flipping is friction. Friction is heat. There is no other trick.

KB-004known issue

Why the middle stays cold

Inside a closed metal box, microwaves reflect and overlap into a fixed pattern of hot and cold points. The turntable isn't a courtesy feature — it's the reason the edge of your plate isn't lava while the center stays lukewarm.

KB-005handling note

The metal rule, precisely

Thin foil, forks, anything with a sharp edge — charge piles up at the point and arcs. A thick, smooth metal surface, like the oven's own walls, just reflects the field harmlessly. That's why the box doing the cooking is metal too.

KB-006emergent behavior

The grape thing is real

Two grape halves, still joined by a strip of skin, microwaved together: the gap between them focuses enough field to strike a small arc of plasma. It's a genuine, published physics demonstration, not an internet myth.

KB-007adoption

From novelty to fixture

What started as restaurant and ship's-galley equipment shrank and cheapened for decades. By the mid-1990s, roughly nine in ten American households had one — quietly, with no launch event.

KB-008lineage

It was defense infrastructure first

The magnetron at the center of every microwave was mass-produced for Allied radar during the Second World War — Raytheon alone built the large majority of the tubes used. The oven is a wartime supply chain with a turntable bolted on.

OC-001

The smallest waves aren't gravity's problem

Ripples under about two centimetres are capillary waves — surface tension pulls them flat, not gravity. Give the wind a little longer and gravity takes over, and you get the swell that actually pushes a boat around.

OC-002

Fetch is the whole game

Wave size mostly comes down to how far the wind has blown over open water, uninterrupted, and for how long. A stiff wind across a pond makes ripples. The same wind across an ocean makes something else entirely.

OC-003

The Beaufort scale grades the sea, not the wind

Each of its thirteen steps, from 0 to 12, was originally written as a description of how the sea surface looks — from glassy calm to foam blown in dense streaks — with a wind speed attached after the fact.

OC-004

"Significant" is a defined number

Significant wave height isn't a judgment call. It's the average height of the tallest third of all the waves passing a point — a specific statistic long before it became a line on a surf forecast.

OC-005

Satellites read the ocean with microwaves

Since NASA's Geos-3 in 1975, radar altimeters in orbit have bounced short microwave pulses off the sea surface and timed the echo, turning that into a global map of wave height. Same physics, pointed the other way.

OC-006

Rogue waves are a separate, real phenomenon

Occasionally a wave arrives roughly twice the height of everything around it, for reasons still debated — wave trains briefly reinforcing each other, or currents focusing energy. One was recorded by sensors on a North Sea platform in 1995 and became the field's benchmark case.

on the naming collision

To be fully transparent: the "micro" in microwave oven refers to the wavelength of the radiation — a few centimetres, tiny next to a radio wave — not the height of anything. It has never, technically, described a small wave. We just liked the sound of the division.
